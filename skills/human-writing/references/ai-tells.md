# AI tells

The patterns that mark prose as machine-generated, grouped by kind. Each entry names the pattern, gives trigger examples, and says what to do instead. The fix is always the same move: find the concrete claim the pattern is hiding and state it.

Most tells are one form of the same default: a model picks the choice that fits the widest range of readers and subjects, where a person picks for one reader and one subject. That is why the fix is always to get specific.

Tells differ in strength. Binary contrasts and negative parallelism, closers that explain the example, vague declaratives and quotables, throat-clearing and signposting, arguing with no one, and chatbot artifacts justify an edit on one sighting. Entries marked *weak alone* are habits careful writers also have; act on them only when other tells sit in the same passage. Clusters are the confession: a "vibrant tapestry" plus a rule of three plus a "Conclusion" section is generated. One stray adverb is not.

These are strong defaults, not bans. The precedence rule in SKILL.md applies: if removing a word changes what the sentence claims, keep it.

## Phrases

### Throat-clearing and signposting

Announcing the point, or the structure, instead of making it.

- Openers: "Here's the thing:", "Here's what/why/this/that [X]", "The truth is,", "The uncomfortable truth is", "It turns out", "Let me be clear", "I'm going to be honest", "Can we talk about", "It goes without saying that", "When it comes to X"
- Meta-commentary: "let's dive in", "here's what you need to know", "in this section we will", "Let me walk you through", "As we'll see", "I want to explore", "The rest of this essay explains", "But that's another post", "Hint:", "Plot twist:", "Spoiler:", "without further ado", "Quick note:"
- Staged candor: a standalone "Honestly?", "Real talk:", "Let's be honest" before a routine claim. "Honestly" inside a casual sentence is ordinary; the staged opener is the tell.

Any "here's what/this/that" construction is throat-clearing. Delete it and do the thing.

### Emphasis crutches and performative sincerity

Words that insist on importance or honesty instead of earning it.

- "Full stop.", "Period.", "Let that sink in.", "Read that again.", "Make no mistake", "This matters because", "Here's why that matters"
- Typographic shouting: one word in ALL CAPS, or periods between words ("every. single. day.").
- "I promise", "You already know this, but", "And that's okay."

Cut entirely.

### Filler, hedges, and empty adverbs

- Filler phrases: "it is important to note that", "It's worth noting", "in order to", "at this point in time", "the ability to", "At the end of the day", "In today's [X]", "In a world where", "The reality is"
- Stacked hedges: "it could potentially possibly", "might arguably", "in some cases it may". Usually a repair of an earlier overstatement, not real doubt. A single ordinary hedge ("perhaps", "tends to") is not a tell.
- Empty adverbs: "really", "just", "literally", "genuinely", "honestly", "simply", "actually", "deeply", "truly", "fundamentally", "inherently", "inevitably", "interestingly", "importantly", "crucially"

Cut, or replace with the one word that means the same. An adverb or qualifier stays when it keeps a claim true ("usually", "in most cases", "roughly").

### Stock AI vocabulary

"delve", "tapestry", "testament", "underscore" (verb), "highlight" (verb), "showcase", "garner", "bolster", "enhance", "intricate", "meticulous", "interplay", "enduring", "pivotal", "crucial", "key" (adjective), "valuable", "additionally", "align with", "quietly". Models use these far more often than people, and a group of them in one passage is strong evidence. Keep technical senses ("a robust estimator", "a feature gate"). The list drifts with every model release; the structural tells below do not.

Replace with the plain word or the specific claim.

### Inflated significance and promotional tone

- Significance: "marks a pivotal moment", "stands/serves as a testament", "plays a vital role", "reflects a broader", "evolving landscape", "leaves an indelible mark", "setting the stage for"
- Brochure adjectives: "vibrant", "rich", "nestled", "in the heart of", "breathtaking", "must-visit", "renowned", "groundbreaking", "seamless", "stunning", "diverse array"
- Stock sections: "Challenges and Legacy", "Future Outlook", "Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive"
- Send-offs: "the future looks bright", "exciting times ahead", "a step in the right direction"

State the fact. Cut the adjective or replace it with the specific. End on the last concrete fact; if the source states real plans, use those.

### Borrowed authority

- Unnamed authorities: "experts argue", "observers have noted", "industry reports", "some critics"
- Prestige lists: "featured in The New York Times, BBC, and Forbes", follower counts, "active social media presence"

A name stands in for what was said. If the source names who said what, use that; otherwise cut the claim or the list. A missing citation alone is not a tell; most writing is unsourced.

### Vague association

"associated with", "in connection with", "linked to", "tied to" where the real relationship is known. "He is associated with the orchestra" hides whether he founded it, conducts it, or bought a ticket. Name the relationship the source gives. If the source does not say, keep the vague wording rather than invent a role.

### Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

"as of my last update", "based on available information", "not widely documented", "while specific details are limited", followed by a plausible guess ("she likely grew up..."). State what the source does not show, or cut the sentence. Never fill the gap.

### Business jargon

| Avoid | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| navigate (challenges) | handle, address |
| unpack | explain, examine |
| lean into | accept, embrace |
| landscape | situation, field |
| game-changer | the specific effect |
| double down | commit, increase |
| deep dive | analysis |
| take a step back | reconsider |
| moving forward | next, from now on |
| circle back | return to, revisit |
| on the same page | aligned, agreed |

### Vague declaratives and quotables

Sentences that announce depth without showing it, or that sound like a pull-quote.

- "The reasons are structural", "The implications are significant", "The stakes are high", "The consequences are real", "This is the deepest problem"
- "X is the language of Y", "X is not a tool but a mirror", "the real question is", "at its core", "what really matters", "X is a feature, not a bug"
- Telling instead of showing: "This is genuinely hard", "This is what leadership actually looks like"

Name the specific reason, implication, or difficulty. If there is none, cut the sentence.

### Arguing with no one

- Defending against an objection nobody raised: "To be clear", "I'm not saying", "Don't get me wrong", "This isn't (mainly) about", "This is not to say"
- Rejecting an option nobody proposed: "A tempting approach would be", "One might be tempted to", "You might think... but", "It would be easy to just"

Usually a leftover from an earlier draft. Remove the defense; if it holds a real claim, state the claim. Keep an objection the text attributes or answers in full, and an option a reader would actually weigh.

### Chatbot artifacts and servility

"Great question!", "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Would you like me to...", "You're absolutely right", "Don't hesitate to reach out". Correspondence pasted as content. Always cut, keeping any real content the wrapper surrounds.

## Structures

### Binary contrasts and negative parallelism

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "Not because X. Because Y." | Telegraphed reversal |
| "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is." | Formulaic reframe |
| "The answer/question isn't X. It's Y." | Predictable pivot |
| "It feels like X. It's actually Y." | Setup and reveal |
| "It's not just X, it's Y" / "not merely X, but Y" / "not just X but also Y" | Additive hedge |
| "This does not mean X. It means Y." | Same contrast split across sentences |
| "stops being X and starts being Y" | False transformation arc |
| Clipped tags hung on a sentence end: "no guessing", "no wasted motion" | Negation as decoration |

State Y directly. Drop the negation. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects a belief the reader actually holds, or when both halves carry information.

### Negative listing

"Not a X... Not a Y... A Z." or "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z." A striptease before the point. State Z.

### Dramatic fragmentation

"[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing].", "X. And Y. And Z.", "This unlocks something. [Word].", "No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia." Manufactured profundity. Merge the fragments into a sentence with a specific claim.

### Closers that explain the example

A sentence after an example, scene, or number that names what it just showed: "This shows the importance of...", "The message was clear:", "It was a lesson in patience." The same closer repeated after several sections ("That is the real win.") is the same tell at paragraph scale. Cut it unless it adds a fact or consequence the example does not show.

### Rhetorical setups

"What if [reframe]?", "Here's what I mean:", "Think about it:", questions answered in the next sentence. Make the point; let the reader draw the conclusion.

### Fake-depth -ing tails

"..., highlighting the...", "..., ensuring...", "..., reflecting...", "..., showcasing...". Participle phrases tacked on to sound analytic. Usually deletable whole.

### Copula avoidance

"serves as", "boasts", "features", "represents" where "is" or "has" would do. Restore the plain verb.

### False agency

Inanimate things doing human verbs, which hides who acted.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "a complaint becomes a fix" | "Support fixed it that week." (if the source says who and when) |
| "the decision emerges" | Someone decides. Name them. |
| "the culture shifts" | People change behavior. |
| "the data tells us" | Someone reads the data and concludes. |
| "the market rewards" | Buyers pay for things. |

Name the human the source names. If it names none and the register allows it, use "you"; otherwise keep a plain neutral subject. Do not invent a team, a role, or a timeline to fill the slot.

### Passive voice and missing subjects

"Mistakes were made", "It is believed that", "The decision was reached". Find the actor and put them at the front. Passive stays when the actor is unknown or irrelevant ("The file is deleted after 30 days"). The same goes for dropped subjects: "No configuration file needed." becomes "You do not need a configuration file." *Weak alone.*

### Narrator from a distance

"Nobody designed this.", "This happens because...", "People tend to...". Lecturer voice floating above the scene. In essays and posts, put the reader in it ("You don't sit down one day and decide to..."). Reference and technical text may stay neutral.

## Document-level patterns

These survive sentence-by-sentence cleanup because every sentence reads fine on its own. Check the shape of the whole text.

### Writing about the document instead of its subject

- Change history outside a changelog: "This function was added to replace the previous approach", "Updated to use..."
- Method narration: "compiled from each vendor's pricing page", "anything we could not confirm is flagged rather than guessed"
- Describing what the reader can see: "the table below compares", "this section is organized by owner"

State the subject. Mention a previous version only in changelogs, release notes, and migration guides. Keep a source credit the reader can follow and a caveat that changes what the reader should do; cut the account of how you worked. One description of the page is *weak alone*.

### Re-explaining what the reader knows

A reply that restates the problem, walks through the diagnosis, and lays out the evidence before it reaches the decision, which sits in the last line. The reader of a reply already has the background. Lead with the decision and keep only what would change whether they agree: usually one fact they lack and any link they need to act. The full diagnosis belongs in the ticket or document. Act on this only when you can see the conversation or the text is plainly a reply.

### Heading echo

The first sentence under a heading restates the heading ("## Performance" then "Speed matters."). Cut the echo and start with the content.

## Rhythm and word patterns

- **Rule of three.** Forced triplets ("innovation, inspiration, and insight"), at sentence scale or as three parallel examples followed by a lesson. Two items often beat three; one often beats two. Keep three when the meaning has three parts.
- **False ranges.** "from X to Y" where X and Y are not a real scale.
- **Synonym cycling.** The same noun renamed every sentence (protagonist, main character, central figure). Pick one.
- **Repeated openings.** Several sentences in a row starting with the same subject ("She noted the door. She noted the lock. She filed both away."). Merge them or begin with the action. Repetition on purpose for rhythm is fine.
- **Metronome sentences.** Three consecutive sentences of similar length. Break one.
- **Punchy endings everywhere.** Every paragraph closing on a one-liner. Vary the endings; often the last sentence is a recap and can go.
- **Staccato stacking.** Short punchy sentences piled up. "Not always. Not perfectly." is hedging dressed as reassurance.
- **Crutch openers.** Sentences starting with What, Why, How ("What makes this hard is...") when the subject could lead ("The constraint is..."). Paragraphs starting with "So" or "Look,".
- **Lazy extremes.** "every", "always", "never", "everyone", "nobody" doing vague work. Use the specific claim.

## Formatting

- Em and en dashes, including spaced double hyphens (` -- `) used as dashes. Use a period, comma, colon, or parentheses. No exceptions in prose; dashes inside code, commands, paths, and URLs stay.
- Curly quotes. Use straight quotes. *Weak alone*, since most editors auto-curl, but always fix.
- Emoji or arrows (→) as decoration on headings or bullets, unless asked for.
- Boldface on every key phrase; bulleted lists where each item is a bold header plus a colon. Turn a labeled list into prose when the labels carry no information of their own.
- Title Case In Headings, and headings written for effect ("The decision, on one screen") instead of naming what the section holds ("How the six options compare").
- A horizontal rule between every section, or an H1 that repeats the document's own title.

Strip to plain text.

## When not to act

Leave a watched phrase alone inside a quotation, a title, a proper name, or a passage that discusses the phrase rather than uses it. Salutations and sign-offs on a letter predate chatbots. A person can make any single choice on this list on purpose; that is why one tell is weak evidence and a cluster is strong.
